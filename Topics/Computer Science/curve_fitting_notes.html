Interpolation vs. Curve Fitting — Why This Chapter Exists

Same raw material — points \((x_i,y_i)\) — two very different jobs.

In Chapter 3 you met interpolation: given exact tabulated values, you built a curve (Newton, Lagrange, …) that passes through every single point. That only makes sense when the data itself is trustworthy — e.g. values of a known function, or precise readings with no noise.

Real experiments are messier. If you measure voltage across a resistor five times, each reading carries small errors from the instrument, human timing, temperature drift, etc. Forcing a curve through all five noisy points would just fit the noise, not the underlying law. Curve fitting instead asks for a curve that runs close to the data — the discrepancy between the curve and the points is made as small as possible, without requiring exact contact anywhere.

Interpolation (Ch. 3)Curve Fitting (Ch. 4)
Data assumedExact, error-freeNoisy / experimental
Curve behaviourPasses through every pointPasses near the points, minimising overall error
Number of points vs. parametersParameters = points (exact match)Points usually >> parameters (over-determined)
Typical useFilling in a value between known, reliable entriesDiscovering the underlying law behind scattered lab/field data
The residual (error). For an approximating function \(Y=f(x)\), the error at the \(i\)-th point is \[ e_i = y_i - f(x_i) \] Curve fitting chooses the free parameters of \(f\) so that these residuals are, collectively, as small as possible — that is the entire idea of the chapter.

The rest of the classical numerical-methods syllabus builds on the same \(e_i\): §4.4 extends it to continuous functions, §4.5 to orthogonal/Chebyshev polynomials, §4.6 to Fourier series — all covered briefly in section 11 below for a complete picture of the chapter.

The Least-Squares Principle

One idea powers every formula in this chapter: minimise the sum of squared errors.

Let the curve \(Y=f(x)\) be fitted to \(m\) data points \((x_i,y_i)\), \(i=1,\dots,m\). Collect all the residuals into one number:

\[ S = e_1^2+e_2^2+\cdots+e_m^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{m}\big[y_i-f(x_i)\big]^2 \tag{4.1–4.2}\]

The method of least squares chooses whatever parameters live inside \(f\) (call them \(a_0,a_1,\dots\)) to make \(S\) as small as possible. Because \(S\) is a smooth function of those parameters, calculus does the rest: set every partial derivative to zero.

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial a_0}=0,\quad \frac{\partial S}{\partial a_1}=0,\quad \dots \]

Each equation you get this way is called a normal equation. Solve the resulting system (linear, if \(f\) is linear in its parameters) and you have the best-fit curve.

Why squares, and not just \(|e_i|\)?
  1. No cancellation. A large positive error and a large negative error would cancel in a plain sum \(\sum e_i\); squaring makes every term positive.
  2. Differentiable everywhere. \(e^2\) is smooth at \(e=0\); \(|e|\) has a sharp corner there, which breaks the "set derivative = 0" trick.
  3. Penalises big misses more. Doubling an error quadruples its contribution to \(S\) — the fit actively avoids leaving any one point far off.
  4. Statistical justification. If the noise in \(y_i\) is independent and normally (Gaussian) distributed, the least-squares solution is exactly the maximum-likelihood estimate of the parameters — this is the link Gauss made (see next section).

Who Devised Least Squares? — Gauss & Legendre

A textbook favourite for a one-mark viva/MCQ question.

Adrien-Marie Legendre (1752–1833)
Was the first to publish the method, in 1805, naming it moindres carrés — "least squares."
Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855)
Published his own treatment in 1809 (Theoria Motus) but claimed to have used it since 1795 to predict the orbit of the asteroid Ceres. He also tied the method to probability, deriving it from the normal (Gaussian) error law.
Exam one-liner: Legendre published first (1805); Gauss gave it a probabilistic foundation and claimed prior use since 1795. Both names are legitimately attached to the method.

4.2.1 Fitting a Straight Line \(Y=a_0+a_1x\)

The workhorse case — master this and every other model in the chapter is a variation on it.

With \(f(x_i)=a_0+a_1x_i\),

\[ S=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big[y_i-(a_0+a_1x_i)\big]^2 \]
Show the derivation of the normal equations

Differentiate \(S\) with respect to each parameter and set the result to zero:

\(\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial a_0}=-2\sum\big[y_i-a_0-a_1x_i\big]=0\ \Rightarrow\ \sum y_i = m\,a_0+a_1\sum x_i\)

\(\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial a_1}=-2\sum x_i\big[y_i-a_0-a_1x_i\big]=0\ \Rightarrow\ \sum x_iy_i = a_0\sum x_i+a_1\sum x_i^2\)

These two simultaneous equations in the unknowns \(a_0,a_1\) are the normal equations.

\[ m\,a_0+a_1\sum x_i=\sum y_i \qquad (4.6a)\] \[ a_0\sum x_i+a_1\sum x_i^2=\sum x_iy_i \qquad (4.6b)\]

Solving this \(2\times2\) system (e.g. by Cramer's rule) gives closed-form formulas — no system-solving needed in practice:

\[ a_1=\frac{m\sum x_iy_i-\sum x_i\sum y_i}{m\sum x_i^2-\left(\sum x_i\right)^2}\qquad (4.7)\] \[ a_0=\bar y-a_1\bar x \qquad (4.8)\]
Because \(a_0=\bar y - a_1\bar x\) rearranges to \(a_0+a_1\bar x=\bar y\), the fitted line always passes through the centroid \((\bar x,\bar y)\) of the data — a fast sanity check on any answer.

Goodness of fit — the correlation coefficient

\[ cc=\sqrt{\frac{S_T-S}{S_T}}\qquad (4.9)\qquad\qquad S_T=\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2\qquad (4.10)\]

\(S_T\) is the total variation of \(y\) about its mean; \(S\) is whatever variation is left over after the line is fitted (using the residuals \(y_i-\hat y_i\), with \(\hat y_i=a_0+a_1x_i\)). So \((S_T-S)/S_T\) is the fraction of the variation the line "explains." \(cc\) close to 1 ⇒ excellent linear fit; close to 0 ⇒ the line explains almost nothing.

Exam recipe for a straight-line fit
  1. Draw a table with columns \(x_i,\ y_i,\ x_i^2,\ x_iy_i\) and sum each column.
  2. Read off \(m,\ \sum x,\ \sum y,\ \sum x^2,\ \sum xy\).
  3. Plug into (4.7) for \(a_1\), then (4.8) for \(a_0\).
  4. If \(cc\) is asked: compute \(\bar y\), then \(S_T=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\) and \(S=\sum(y_i-a_0-a_1x_i)^2\) (extra table columns), then apply (4.9).
  5. State the fitted equation and interpret the slope/intercept in the problem's own units.

4.2.2 Multiple Linear Least Squares

Same idea, one more predictor: fit \(z=a_0+a_1x+a_2y\) to triples \((x_i,y_i,z_i)\).

Minimising \(S=\sum(z_i-a_0-a_1x_i-a_2y_i)^2\) and setting all three partial derivatives to zero gives three normal equations:

\[ m\,a_0+a_1\sum x_i+a_2\sum y_i=\sum z_i \] \[ a_0\sum x_i+a_1\sum x_i^2+a_2\sum x_iy_i=\sum x_iz_i \] \[ a_0\sum y_i+a_1\sum x_iy_i+a_2\sum y_i^2=\sum y_iz_i \qquad (4.11)\]
General pattern: a model linear in its parameters — with k predictors — always yields k+1 linear normal equations, one per parameter. In matrix form this is the familiar (XᵀX)a = Xᵀz, where \(X\) is the "design matrix" whose first column is all 1's, followed by one column per predictor — exactly the same object behind multiple regression in statistics.

4.2.3 Linearization of Nonlinear Laws

Most "curve fitting" problems you'll actually be given are not straight lines — but an algebraic substitution turns them into one.

If a nonlinear law can be rearranged into the form \(Y=A_0+A_1X\) for some new variables \(X,Y\) built from the original \(x,y\), then the straight-line formulas (4.7)–(4.8) apply directly to the transformed data. Fit the line, then convert \(A_0,A_1\) back into the original constants.

LawLinearised formSubstitution
\(y=ax+\dfrac{b}{x}\)\(xy=ax^2+b\)\(Y=xy,\ X=x^2\)
\(xy^a=b\)\(\log x+a\log y=\log b\)\(Y=\log y,\ X=\log x\)
\(y=ab^x\)\(\log y=\log a+x\log b\)\(A_0=\log a,\ A_1=\log b\)
\(y=ax^b\)\(\log y=\log a+b\log x\)\(A_0=\log a,\ A_1=b,\ X=\log x\)
\(y=ae^{bx}\)\(\ln y=\ln a+bx\)\(A_0=\ln a,\ A_1=b,\ X=x\)
\(y=\dfrac{x}{a+bx}\)\(\dfrac{1}{y}=b+a\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\)\(Y=1/y,\ X=1/x\)

Two more you'll meet in practice, by the same idea: \(y=\dfrac{1}{a+bx}\) inverts directly to \(\dfrac1y=a+bx\) (so \(Y=1/y,\ X=x\)); and \(y=a+\dfrac{b}{x}\) is already linear in \(1/x\) (so \(X=1/x\) with no other change needed). Any law you can algebraically bend into "constant + constant × (something)" is fair game for the line-fit machinery.

4.2.4 Curve Fitting by Polynomials

When the data bends more than once, or a straight line just won't do — fit a polynomial.

\[ Y=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+\cdots+a_nx^n \qquad (4.12)\]

Minimising \(S\) gives \(n+1\) normal equations (4.14). For the common case of a parabola (\(n=2\)):

\[ m\,a_0+a_1\sum x+a_2\sum x^2=\sum y\] \[ a_0\sum x+a_1\sum x^2+a_2\sum x^3=\sum xy\] \[ a_0\sum x^2+a_1\sum x^3+a_2\sum x^4=\sum x^2y\]
Watch out: for large \(n\) the coefficient matrix becomes ill-conditioned — the power columns \(1,x,x^2,x^3,\dots\) start to look increasingly alike, so tiny rounding errors in the data blow up into large swings in the \(a_i\). In practice, orthogonal (Chebyshev) polynomials are used instead for high-degree fits — see section 11.

4.2.5 Sum of Exponentials — Overview

A model that can't be linearised by a single log — worth recognising, rarely examined in full.

A model like \(y=Ae^{r_1x}+Be^{r_2x}+\cdots\) is nonlinear in the rate parameters \(r_1,r_2,\dots\) — because it's a sum of exponentials rather than a single power or exponential, no single logarithm turns it into a straight line. Two classical ways around this:

  • Prony's method — for equally-spaced data, converts the fitting problem into finding the roots of a polynomial built from the data, which is a linear-algebra problem in disguise.
  • Nonlinear least squares — start from a guess and refine iteratively (Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt); this is what most software (Excel Solver, scipy, MATLAB lsqcurvefit) actually does.
Exam scope note: recognise the model and know why it resists direct linearisation — full derivations are normally beyond an introductory course.

4.3 Weighted Least Squares

Not all data points deserve equal trust — let the fit know that.

If some points come from a more precise instrument, more repeated trials, or are otherwise known to be more reliable, we can attach a weight \(W_i>0\) to each point and minimise a weighted sum of squared errors instead:

\[ S=\sum_{i} W_i\big[y_i-f(x_i)\big]^2 \qquad (4.26)\]

If every \(W_i=1\) this collapses back to ordinary least squares — weighting is a generalisation, not a different technique. For a line \(Y=a_0+a_1x\), every sum in the normal equations simply picks up a factor of \(W_i\):

\[ a_0\sum W_i+a_1\sum W_ix_i=\sum W_iy_i \qquad (4.31)\] \[ a_0\sum W_ix_i+a_1\sum W_ix_i^2=\sum W_ix_iy_i \qquad (4.32)\]

The same trick extends to a weighted polynomial fit — every sum in (4.14) gains a \(W_i\):

\[ S=\sum W_i\big[y_i-(a_0+a_1x_i+\cdots+a_nx_i^n)\big]^2 \qquad (4.34)\]

Beyond §4.3 — Completing the Chapter (§4.4–4.6)

These sections extend the same idea further; Sastry treats them after weighted least squares. Brief overviews only — check your syllabus for how much depth is examined.

§4.4 Continuous Data

Instead of a finite list of points, approximate a continuous function \(f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) by minimising \(\int_a^b [f(x)-\varphi(x)]^2\,w(x)\,dx\) over a polynomial \(\varphi\). The discrete sums \(\sum x_i^k\) are simply replaced by integrals (moments) \(\int x^k w(x)\,dx\) — the same normal-equation idea, continuous version.

§4.5 Chebyshev / Orthogonal Polynomials

The power basis \(1,x,x^2,\dots\) gets ill-conditioned fast (see §4.2.4's warning). Using orthogonal polynomials as the basis instead makes the normal-equation matrix diagonal — each coefficient is found independently, with no ill-conditioning. A related idea, minimax approximation, minimises the worst-case error instead of the sum of squares; its optimal error curve famously oscillates equally between + and − extremes (the equioscillation property).

§4.6 Fourier Approximation & FFT

For periodic data, fit \(a_0+\sum(a_k\cos kx+b_k\sin kx)\). Sines and cosines are mutually orthogonal over one period, so the normal equations decouple into a simple independent formula for every \(a_k,b_k\) — no matrix solve at all. The Fast Fourier Transform computes all these coefficients for \(n\) points in \(O(n\log n)\) time instead of \(O(n^2)\), which is why it underlies practically all digital signal processing.

Everything on one screen for last-minute review.

Core principle

\(S=\sum e_i^2,\quad e_i=y_i-f(x_i)\)

Straight line

\(a_1=\dfrac{m\sum xy-\sum x\sum y}{m\sum x^2-(\sum x)^2}\)

\(a_0=\bar y-a_1\bar x\)

Correlation

\(cc=\sqrt{\dfrac{S_T-S}{S_T}},\ S_T=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\)

Multiple linear (3 eqns)

\(m a_0+a_1\sum x+a_2\sum y=\sum z\)
\(a_0\sum x+a_1\sum x^2+a_2\sum xy=\sum xz\)
\(a_0\sum y+a_1\sum xy+a_2\sum y^2=\sum yz\)

Weighted (line)

\(a_0\sum W+a_1\sum Wx=\sum Wy\)
\(a_0\sum Wx+a_1\sum Wx^2=\sum Wxy\)

Linearisation quick table

\(y=ae^{bx}\to \ln y=\ln a+bx\)

\(y=ax^b\to \log y=\log a+b\log x\)

\(y=ab^x\to \log y=\log a+x\log b\)

Polynomial (degree n)

\(n+1\) normal equations; parabola needs sums up to \(\sum x^4\) and \(\sum x^2y\).

Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Sign slip in the derivation. Both partial derivatives start with a factor of \(-2\) — dropping the sign flips every normal equation.
  • Mixing up \(a_0\) and \(a_1\). Only \(a_1\) needs the "cross term" \(m\sum xy-\sum x\sum y\); \(a_0\) is simply \(\bar y-a_1\bar x\) once \(a_1\) is known.
  • Wrong \(S\) inside \(cc\). \(S_T\) uses \(\bar y\); \(S\) uses the fitted \(\hat y_i=a_0+a_1x_i\), not the raw \(y_i\) again.
  • Forgetting to convert back. After linearising, \(A_0,A_1\) are properties of the transformed line — always convert back (e.g. \(a=e^{A_0}\), not \(A_0\) itself).
  • Wrong equation count for polynomials. Degree \(n\) needs exactly \(n+1\) normal equations, with sums running up to \(\sum x^{2n}\).
  • Dropping \(W_i\) from the plain-sum equation. In weighted problems, even the "\(\sum W_i\)" term in the first normal equation must carry the weights — a very common miss.
  • Rounding too early. Carry 4–5 decimal places through intermediate sums; round only the final reported answer.
